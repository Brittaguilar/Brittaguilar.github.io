AI for Audio Journalists

A practical guide for people who make audio: what these tools actually are, what they are useful for, what newsrooms have already tried, and where it goes wrong. Claude is used for the worked examples, but almost everything here applies to any of them.

00 — How AI works

A prediction machine, not a library

You can do excellent work with these tools without touching the mathematics underneath. What is worth having is an accurate working model of what the thing is doing, because that is what lets you predict where it will be reliable and where it will fail quietly. This page builds that model, and the reading at the end takes it further.

How it builds an answer, one word at a time

A large language model reads everything in front of it. Then it produces a ranked list of the words that might come next. It picks one from near the top of that list and adds it to the end.

Then it reads the whole thing again, including the word it just added, and picks the next one. That is the entire mechanism at the point where you use it.

It reads chunks of text, not words

It sees tokens. A token is a chunk of text: sometimes a whole word, sometimes part of one. "Woods Hole" might arrive as three separate chunks. Because the model never sees individual letters, it can be surprisingly bad at counting them or spelling a word backward, while being fluent about everything else. You can watch text get chopped up at Tiktokenizer.

Training it and using it are separate things

But your conversations may still be stored

Your conversation does not change the model while you are working. But it may be stored, and it may be used to train some future version. That is a separate question, and on most consumer plans it is a setting you control. It is a privacy question rather than a mechanism one, so tab 04 covers it.

Where the answer comes from: your material, or its training

An answer can come from either of two places. They are not equally reliable, and learning to tell them apart is the most useful thing on this page.

Why it makes things up

It is not broken, and it is not lying. It is doing the one thing it does, which is producing the most plausible next words. A citation that looks right is easier to produce than one that is right, so that is often what you get. There are two good explanations for this, and they fit together rather than competing.

The mechanism

Anthropic's interpretability team found that saying "I don't know" is the model's default. Something has to switch that default off: an internal signal meaning roughly "I recognize this name." Sometimes the signal fires for a name the model knows nothing about. The refusal turns off, and the model "proceeds to confabulate: to generate a plausible — but unfortunately untrue — response."

Tracing the thoughts of a large language model
The incentive

OpenAI's argument is about incentives. The tests used to score these models give credit for a confident guess and nothing for "I don't know" — the same way a multiple-choice exam rewards a student who never leaves a blank. So guessing gets trained in. Worth noting that this is a lab writing about its own product category.

Why language models hallucinate

Both point to the same fix. Saying in the prompt that "not in the material" is an acceptable answer changes what counts as a good answer, and the model responds to that. Every prompt in tab 06 carries some version of that line.

Four things to remember

  1. It works better with the material in front of it

    Give it the document rather than relying on what it might remember. Every time.

  2. A confident tone tells you nothing

    It sounds exactly as sure when it is inventing as when it is right. There is no tell in the prose, so the writing itself will not warn you.

  3. Exact words are checkable; summaries are not

    A verbatim quote is something you can go and find in the source. A summary is something you have to take on trust.

  4. Running it twice is a cheap test

    Ask the same question again in a fresh chat. If the two answers disagree, the first one was less solid than it sounded.

01 — Start here

Getting set up

The practical constraints are the part that is least written about: what these tools will accept, what they cost, and how to set one up so you are not re-explaining your show every time you open it. That is this tab.

What it can read, and what it can't

These tools read text, documents and images. None of the mainstream chat assistants will take a WAV, an MP3 or a video file and listen to it for you.

So every audio workflow in this guide starts the same way. You transcribe the tape somewhere else first, then give the model the transcript.

Choosing a tool

The mechanism in tab 00 is the same across Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and the open models you can run on your own machine. The differences that matter for this work are mundane and worth checking before you commit a workflow to one of them.

  • What it will accept. How many files per conversation, how large, and how many pages of PDF. This decides whether your actual transcripts fit.
  • How much it can hold at once. A 70-minute transcript is perhaps 12,000 words. A records dump is not. Ask how much text the tool can consider in one go.
  • Whether your conversations train the model by default, and whether you can turn that off. On consumer plans this is usually a setting; on business plans it is usually off already.
  • Whether a persistent workspace exists. Claude calls these Projects. Other tools have their own version. Without one, you re-explain your show in every conversation.
  • Whether it can be run locally. For material that could put someone at risk, a model on your own machine is the answer, and open models are good enough for transcript work now. ProPublica did this for Uvalde →

Everything below uses Claude for the concrete steps, because a worked example is more useful than a survey. The moves transfer.

Where to start, by size of shop

One person

Tape logging on a piece you are already making is the natural first move. Free tier, one Project, one real interview. The payoff is hours, and the limits show up fast.

The document material can wait until you have a records story, and the policy work until you publish something with AI in the process.

Small team or station

The policy is the better starting point here, not the tool. Agreeing what may be pasted in and what must be disclosed, before anyone builds a habit, takes an afternoon and prevents a retrofit later.

After that, one person piloting tape logging and writing up what happened will teach the room more than five people experimenting quietly.

Network or newsroom

The binding constraint here is confidentiality, not capability. Business plans where training is off by default are worth looking at, as are self-hosted models for anything sensitive.

The boring shared thing — a monitoring pipeline over the audio your beat reporters cannot listen to — is where every documented newsroom win has come from.

Your first hour

  1. Privacy settings, before anything gets pasted

    In Claude the setting lives under Settings, then Privacy. With the option that lets your conversations improve the model turned off, your chats are kept until you delete them rather than held for up to five years in training pipelines. For anything sensitive, an Incognito chat is worth using on top of that — those are never used to improve the model.

  2. A workspace for one real show

    Real material shows you the limits faster than a test case does, so the show you are working on this week is the better choice. In Claude this is a Project: a folder holding its own chats, its own uploaded files and its own standing instructions, so you never re-explain the show.

  3. Your standards, in the instructions

    Two paragraphs covers it: how you name things, what the show sounds like, and one explicit line — never invent a quote; if a quote is not word for word in the material I gave you, say so. Use the instructions prompt →

  4. Three things you already have

    A finished script you are proud of, your style guide, and one transcript. That is the whole setup — nothing else is needed to start.

  5. The kind of question you'd give a sharp intern

    "Here is 90 minutes of tape. Log it in two-minute chunks with a one-line summary of each, and flag the six moments where her voice changes." Then go and listen to those six moments. Use the tape-log prompt →

What a good prompt looks like

What separates a useless answer from a useful one is nearly always specificity. Say what the material is. Say what job you want done. Say what shape the answer should come back in.

RoleYou are an experienced radio producer logging tape for a 22-minute narrative feature.
MaterialThe attached file is a Whisper transcript of a 71-minute interview with a commercial fisher in New Bedford. Timecodes are approximate.
JobBuild me a tape log. Flag every passage where she describes a specific event with a date, and every passage with strong scene-setting sound described in the room.
ConstraintQuote verbatim from the transcript only. If you are unsure whether something is a direct quote, mark it UNCERTAIN rather than guessing.
OutputA table: timecode · topic · verbatim pull quote · why it matters · confidence.

Why the constraint line matters

Telling the model it is allowed to say "I don't know" cuts down on invention. ProPublica writes that permission into its prompts as standard practice.

02 — Tape and documents

Sorting the pile, not interpreting it

Seventy minutes of tape and seven hundred pages of records are the same problem wearing different clothes: more material than you can read, and the part that matters buried in it. The move is the same for both, and so is the rule that makes it publishable.

Every newsroom that has published a methodology note describes this same division of labor, in nearly the same words. The machine ranks and routes the material. The person reads all of what the machine hands over. Nothing in the middle counts as a finding.

The same move, four orders of magnitude apart

These are the pile sizes newsrooms have actually worked through with this method. The units are not comparable to each other — that is rather the point. The technique does not change between the smallest and the largest.

Six techniques that work at any size

A citation with every extraction

"Which of these mention X" is the weaker version of this question. The stronger one asks for the exact sentence that made it flag each document. Without that there is nothing to check against, and you are trusting a summary of something you have not read.

ProPublica, 3,400 grants →
Hand classification first, AI as the second pass

Read and classify by hand first. Only then run a model over the same pile, as a second pass to catch your own mistakes. If you plan to publish a number, this is the most defensible way to get there.

New York Times, 10,000 SEC documents →
Room to say "I don't know"

Writing that permission into the prompt, along with two or three confirmed examples of what you are looking for, gives it something to calibrate against. An answer that flags its own uncertainty is worth more than a confident one you cannot test.

A definition that sharpens each pass

Your first definition of what counts as interesting will be wrong. Run it, read what comes back, sharpen the definition, run it again. Three passes beats one careful one.

iTromsø, hospital records →
Cross-referencing for what is missing

Compare what you were given against a separate source that says what should exist. The gaps are the story, and each one becomes your next records request.

Sensitive material kept in-house

If the documents could put someone at risk, run an open model on your own machine instead of pasting into a consumer chatbot. The material never leaves the newsroom.

Logging an interview, step by step

Why quotes need checking against the audio

Whisper is the speech-to-text engine behind most do-it-yourself transcription setups, and behind the BBC's own Sounds subtitle trial. It sometimes fabricates sentences nobody said, most often in silences and other stretches of non-speech audio. If one of those inventions is sitting in your transcript, the model will read it as real and quote it straight back to you, and nothing on the page will look wrong. The study on non-speech-induced hallucinations · CNTI's briefing on the risk

  1. Transcribing with timecodes on

    Whatever tool you use, timestamps and speaker labels are worth turning on at export. The speaker labels will be unreliable, and results get worse with strong regional accents — newsrooms running this at scale report both problems.

  2. The transcript goes into the show's workspace

    Not a fresh chat. Inside the workspace it sits alongside your style guide and your standards line, so you do not have to restate either.

  3. A log, not a summary

    A summary throws away exactly what you need, which is the words and where to find them. A table with five columns keeps it: timecode, topic, verbatim quote, why it earns space, and how confident the model is. Use the tape-log prompt →

  4. A second question the transcript can answer and you can't

    Things like: "Where does she contradict what she said earlier?" Or "list every mention of a date or a dollar figure." Across several interviews at once: "In these six transcripts, who describes the same night differently?" Use the contradictions prompt →

  5. Checking every select against the audio

    Timecode by timecode, there are two things to check. First, that the words on the page are what was actually said. Second, that the delivery matches what the text led you to expect. The second one is the check only you can do.

  6. Structure, once the selects are verified

    Once your selects are verified, the model is a reasonable sounding board on running order and act breaks. Nobody has documented doing this well yet, so treat it as an experiment. Use the selects prompt →

Working through documents, step by step

  1. A workspace for the story

    Documents added as workspace knowledge, rather than as chat attachments, stay visible to every conversation in the workspace. One detail worth knowing: for PDFs under 100 pages Claude reads the images as well as the text, which matters for scanned records and handwriting.

  2. A map of the corpus, before any real question

    Asking what is in the set — document types, date range, recurring names, anything obviously missing — confirms it read what you think it read, before you rely on it. Use the corpus-map prompt →

  3. Two sentences on what you are looking for

    Those two sentences become the definition you hand the model, with a couple of examples alongside. Vague criteria produce vague flags, and the difference is hard to spot after the fact.

  4. A table, with a source in every row

    One row per hit: document name, page, verbatim quote, why it matched, and confidence. A row with no quote you can go and find is not worth reading. Use the forced-citation prompt →

  5. The original, for every row you keep

    Every one, not a sample. This is the step that every Pulitzer AI disclosure describes, and the one that makes the work publishable.

  6. The timeline, once the rows are verified

    Once the rows are verified, you can safely ask for a chronology built from them. The inputs are now yours rather than the model's, so the output is checkable against your own work. Use the chronology prompt →

A useful way to picture the work

The New York Times's internal tool, Cheatsheet, is just a spreadsheet. Each row is a document or a transcript. Each column is one small job for the model: extract the quotes, classify it, translate it, summarize it, search the web about it. Columns can feed into each other. You can build a crude version in any spreadsheet today, and the Times says it plans to open-source the real one.

03 — Running the show

The work that isn't journalism

Pitch piles, guest research, show notes, chapter markers, the grant report nobody wants to write. None of it is journalism, and all of it eats the week. It is also the lowest-risk place to start, because nothing here goes to air.

Setting up a show workspace

  1. What a new producer would need on day one

    Your format doc, your style guide, three scripts that represent the show at its best, and your standards memo. Together that is the show bible. The model now has it in every conversation, without you pasting anything again.

  2. Instructions written in the second person

    "You are working on [show], a 28-minute narrative feature for public radio. Sentences are short. We do not use the word 'delve'. Never invent a quote or a fact; if the answer is not in the material, say so." Use the instructions prompt →

  3. Recurring chores as their own chats

    Keep one chat for pitch triage and go back to it every week. Keep another for show notes. Each one builds up a sense of how you like the answer, which a fresh chat would lose.

  4. Connectors, only where they earn it

    Connectors for Google Drive, Gmail and Calendar are available on all Claude plans. When you connect one, the model gets the same level of access you have. So connect a single folder rather than your whole working life.

04 — Guardrails

What not to paste, and what to tell your audience

A chatbot account is a company holding your notes. Treat the text box the way you would treat a phone call on a line you do not control.

What you should never paste in

source identities off-the-record material unpublished sensitive documents legally privileged material contracts & correspondence anything you promised to protect indefinitely

The Freedom of the Press Foundation's rule is the clearest one: do not submit anything you would not want the company to see, or would not want exposed. There are two concrete reasons behind it. The company can access what you send, and a court can compel it to hand that over. And depending on your settings, what you send may be used to train the model.

How long your data is kept

Every tool answers this differently, and the answer changes. Claude's, as an example: on the consumer plans — Free, Pro and Max — you choose whether your conversations are used to improve the model. Decline, and chats are kept until you delete them. Accept, and your data may also be held for up to five years in training pipelines. A chat you delete leaves your history right away, and back-end storage within 30 days.

Two caveats. Conversations that automated safety systems flag are kept for up to two years whatever you chose. And no consumer plan can get a zero-retention arrangement; those are for approved enterprise customers only.

Deletion promises can be overridden by a court

In 2025 a court ordered OpenAI to preserve all ChatGPT output data indefinitely. That included deleted chats, which would normally have gone within 30 days, across Free, Plus, Pro and Team accounts. The order ended in September 2025, but everything captured in that window is still under legal hold. A company's retention policy is a policy, not a guarantee. OpenAI's own account

Writing your own policy

  1. Poynter's template as the starting point

    It already contains the clauses on sources, disclosure, verification and voice likeness. You are editing a draft rather than writing one.

  2. WBEZ's audio clauses, worth taking word for word

    It is the most audio-specific policy any public radio organization has published. It covers text-to-speech, translation and voice cloning explicitly, which most policies do not.

  3. One disclosure line, decided in advance

    Write the sentence you would put in a show description or read on air. Do it now, so it is not being drafted under deadline pressure.

    A policy about protecting confidential material only holds if the training setting is actually switched off.

05 — The evidence

What has actually been done, and what hasn't

Every case here was either published by the newsroom itself or reported by a journalism research organization. Where a newsroom described what its people did by hand, that part is included. It is usually the more useful half. The techniques in the other tabs link back to these.

Audio and broadcast

The New York Times Feb 2026 · podcast monitoring ~80 podcasts, nightly

The system downloads a hand-picked list of shows, transcribes them, summarizes each one, then emails about 40 reporters a single summary of the talking points they had in common, at 8am. It fed coverage of the Epstein-files backlash and the Sydney Sweeney ad story. Reporters always go back to the source audio before using anything.

Nieman Lab
Michigan Radio (WUOM) Dec 2023 · public meetings ~2,000 transcripts · 100+ bodies

Reporter Dustin Dwyer built a pipeline that transcribes local government meeting audio into a searchable database, with timestamped links back to the audio and keyword email alerts. His own verdict on the AI summaries: "inconsistent." The transferable lesson is his framing — spend the effort structuring the dataset, so whatever tool comes next can be pointed at it.

Current
Chalkbeat Mar 2025 · meeting audio ~80 school districts · 8 bureaus

Runs keyword alerts across transcribed school-board meetings using a vendor tool, LocalLens. One reporter found a source for a story about LGBTQ+ students from a single alert. Reported failure modes worth knowing: poor speaker identification, trouble with regional accents, and everything still needing independent verification.

A three-month trial generating subtitles and transcripts with Whisper across five shows, with editorial staff reviewing and editing every one before publication. Reviewed afterward on accuracy and cost.

Sen. Cruz's office had flagged the grants as advancing "neo-Marxist class warfare." A small Norwegian paper broke a hospital records release into chunks, queried it for anomalies, and refined its definition of "newsworthy" on each pass. It found a doctor who had spent four seconds reviewing an X-ray. The story won Norway's Data-SKUP award, and the paper estimates it avoided more than three months of manual work.

KQED, 40 other newsrooms and UC Berkeley. AI summarizes files, pulls out incident dates, classifies case types and groups loose documents into coherent cases. People review every case flagged as misconduct. The system also cross-references against state data to find records that agencies never produced — which generates the next round of requests.

What nobody has documented

This list is the honest other half of the gallery above. Each of these is a gap we looked for and did not find, as of September 2026.

  • Script structure. No documented case of a named producer using a language model for act breaks, cold opens or running order in journalistic audio. Vendors claim it constantly. No newsroom has written up how it went. If you try it, you are ahead of the evidence.
  • A mechanism explainer for journalists. Poynter, Nieman Lab, the Reuters Institute, RJI and JournalismAI all publish on AI ethics, policy and tools. None of them explains how the models work. Tab 00 exists because that gap does.
  • An audio-journalism customer story. Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI has published one for any audio-journalism organization.
  • Anything from Transom. Transom has published nothing on AI. The Transom 25 program was about method rather than tools, which is consistent with that.
  • Public-radio policies beyond one. No published AI policy found for PRX, Radiotopia, KQED or New York Public Radio. NPR's guidelines exist internally but are not public — the URL that circulates for them returns a 404. Chicago Public Media's is the benchmark by default.
  • Two items on this page are second-hand. The CADA AI-host story was broken by the Australian press and could not be verified against a primary source here. The New York Times methodology notes are paywalled, so they are described through IRE, Nieman Lab and iMEdD Lab.
06 — Prompts

Starting points, not finished prompts

Each of these assumes your material is already attached, or sitting in a workspace. They are worth editing before use — a prompt written in your show's own vocabulary works better than a generic one. Every one of them carries a line giving the model permission to say it doesn't know.

For logging tape

Tape log with confidence flags

You are an experienced radio producer logging tape for a narrative feature.

The attached file is a machine transcript of a [LENGTH] interview with [WHO, ONE LINE OF CONTEXT]. Timecodes are approximate and the transcription may contain errors.

Build a tape log as a table with these columns:
timecode | topic | verbatim quote | why it earns space | confidence

Rules:
- Quote verbatim from the transcript only. Never smooth, trim or paraphrase inside quotation marks.
- If you are not certain a passage is a direct quote, mark confidence LOW and say why.
- Flag separately any passage where the speaker describes a specific event with a date, place or figure.
- Do not summarize the interview. I want the log.

Used in 01 · Start here and 02 · Tape and documents

Selects for a specific length

From this transcript, propose the strongest 4 minutes of tape for a [LENGTH] piece about [SUBJECT].

For each select give me: in-point and out-point timecodes, the verbatim text, its running time estimate at normal speech, and one sentence on what it does for the story that nothing else does.

Rank them. Then tell me which two you would cut first if I needed to lose 90 seconds, and why.

If two selects make the same point, say so rather than offering both.

Used in 02 · Tape and documents

Contradictions across several interviews

These [N] transcripts are interviews with different people about [EVENT].

Find every place where two or more accounts disagree on a fact: a time, a sequence, who was present, who said what, a number.

For each disagreement give me: the fact in dispute, each speaker's verbatim words with their timecode, and how material the difference is.

Do not resolve the disagreements or guess who is right. List them.

Used in 02 · Tape and documents

For documents

Map the corpus before you question it

Before I ask you anything substantive about these documents, describe what you actually have.

List: document types and roughly how many of each, the date range, the recurring names and organizations, the apparent gaps (missing dates, referenced attachments that are not here, redactions), and anything unreadable.

Be specific about what you cannot read. That matters more to me than what you can.

Used in 02 · Tape and documents

Flag with a forced citation

Go through these documents and find every instance of [YOUR CRITERION, IN TWO SENTENCES].

Here are two confirmed examples of what I mean: [EXAMPLE 1]. [EXAMPLE 2].

Return one row per hit: document name | page or section | the verbatim sentence that justifies the flag | why it matched | confidence high/medium/low.

You must include the verbatim sentence. If you cannot locate one, do not include the row.

If you are unsure whether something qualifies, include it and mark it LOW rather than deciding for me. If you find nothing, say so — do not pad the list.

Used in 02 · Tape and documents

Chronology from verified items

Here are [N] items I have independently verified. Build a chronology.

Columns: date | what happened | source document | who was involved | what is still unknown at this point in the timeline.

Where two items conflict on a date, show both rows and mark the conflict. Do not interpolate events that are not in the material.

Used in 02 · Tape and documents

For running the show

Project instructions for a show

You are working on [SHOW], a [LENGTH] [FORMAT] for [AUDIENCE].

Voice: [TWO SENTENCES — sentence length, register, what the show never does].
Never use: [YOUR BANNED WORDS].
Names and style: [HOW YOU HANDLE TITLES, PLACES, PRONUNCIATION NOTES].

Standards, non-negotiable:
- Never invent a quote, a name, a date or a source. If something is not in the material I have given you, say "not in the material."
- When you are uncertain, say so in the answer rather than hedging in the prose.
- Anything you produce is a draft for a human to verify, not output.
- If I ask you for something that would breach these, tell me.

Used in 01 · Start here and 03 · Running the show

Pitch triage

Here are [N] pitches. Sort them against what this show actually makes — you have the format doc and past scripts.

For each: one-line summary | closest past episode | what is missing before this could be commissioned | reporting lift, low/medium/high.

Then group them: obvious yes, needs a conversation, wrong show. Put anything you are unsure about in "needs a conversation" rather than guessing.

Do not rewrite the pitches. Do not be encouraging.

Used in 03 · Running the show

Show notes and chapters from a verified log

Using the attached verified tape log — not the raw transcript — write:

1. Chapter markers with timecodes, each title under six words, in the show's voice.
2. A 40-word episode description.
3. A 240-word show-notes block with the guest's title and affiliation as they appear in the log, and any resources mentioned on tape.

Every name, title and figure must come from the log. Flag anything you had to leave blank.

Used in 03 · Running the show

Pre-interview brief with sources

Search the web and build me a pre-interview brief on [PERSON], who I am interviewing about [TOPIC].

Give me: what they are known for, what they have said publicly on this topic and where, any position they have changed, and the three questions they are always asked so I can avoid them.

Cite a source for every factual claim. Where you could not find a source, say "unsourced" rather than filling it in. I will be checking the citations.

Used in 03 · Running the show

How to tell a prompt is working

A good prompt makes the model's uncertainty visible to you. If everything comes back confident and tidy, it probably has not been given permission to fail — adding the line that lets it say "not in the material" and running it again usually shows the difference.
